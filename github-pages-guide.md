# H. Pylori: Complete Patient Guide

> A plain-language guide to Helicobacter pylori infection — what it is, how it is found, and how it is treated. Published for patient education.

---

## What is H. pylori?

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a spiral-shaped bacterium that lives in the lining of the stomach. It is one of the most common chronic bacterial infections in the world, and prevalence in Pakistan is among the highest globally — studies consistently report that a large majority of adults carry it, often without knowing.

The bacterium survives stomach acid by producing an enzyme called urease, which neutralises acid around it. Over time, it triggers chronic inflammation of the stomach lining (gastritis). Most infected people never develop symptoms, but in a significant minority the infection leads to ulcers, and it is a recognised risk factor for gastric cancer over decades.

## How do people get it?

H. pylori spreads through contaminated food and water and through close person-to-person contact, usually within families. Infection is typically acquired in childhood in regions with limited sanitation. It does not spread through casual contact like handshakes, but sharing utensils and poor hand hygiene within households can transmit it.

## Symptoms

Most carriers have no symptoms at all. When symptoms occur, they usually relate to gastritis or ulcers:

- A dull or burning ache in the upper abdomen, often worse on an empty stomach
- Bloating, frequent burping, and a feeling of fullness after small meals
- Nausea, and occasionally vomiting
- Loss of appetite and unexplained weight loss
- In ulcer cases: black or tarry stools, or vomiting that looks like coffee grounds — both require urgent medical attention

**Important:** these symptoms overlap heavily with acid reflux, functional dyspepsia, and gallbladder disease. Symptoms alone cannot diagnose H. pylori — testing is required.

## How is it tested?

Several reliable tests exist, and the choice depends on the clinical situation:

1. **Stool antigen test** — detects H. pylori proteins in a stool sample. Non-invasive, widely available, and accurate when done correctly.
2. **Urea breath test** — you drink a urea solution and breathe into a bag; the bacterium's urease activity produces a detectable signal. Highly accurate.
3. **Blood antibody test** — simple but cannot distinguish active infection from a past one, so it is less useful for confirming cure.
4. **Endoscopy with biopsy** — recommended when there are alarm symptoms (bleeding, weight loss, difficulty swallowing) or when ulcers are suspected. A small tissue sample is tested directly.

**Before testing:** proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), antibiotics, and bismuth must be stopped 1–2 weeks beforehand, as they suppress the bacterium and cause false negatives. Your doctor will advise on timing.

## How is it treated?

The standard treatment is a 10–14 day course of combination therapy: a PPI to reduce acid plus two or three antibiotics. Common regimens include clarithromycin-based triple therapy or bismuth-based quadruple therapy, chosen based on local antibiotic resistance patterns.

Key points about treatment:

- **Finish the full course.** Stopping early because symptoms improve is the main cause of treatment failure and antibiotic resistance.
- **Side effects** (metallic taste, mild nausea, loose stools) are common but usually manageable — report severe ones to your doctor.
- **Confirm eradication.** A stool antigen or breath test 4+ weeks after finishing treatment confirms the bacterium is gone. This step is frequently skipped and should not be.
- **Household members** with similar symptoms should be tested too, since reinfection within families occurs.

## Can it be prevented?

Good hygiene is the main defence: safe drinking water, thorough handwashing, and properly cooked food. There is no vaccine. Once eradicated, reinfection rates are low in adults with good sanitation.

## When to see a specialist

See a gastroenterologist if you have persistent upper-abdominal symptoms, a family history of stomach ulcers or gastric cancer, or if a previous H. pylori treatment failed. Failed eradication needs a different antibiotic combination — repeating the same regimen rarely works.
